Solar for a 2 BHK or 3 BHK Home: How Many kW, Panels and Cost

"How much solar does a 3 BHK need?" has no single answer, because two flats of the same size can use very different amounts of electricity. What decides the size is the units on your bill, and in most homes the biggest swing is how many air conditioners run and for how long. This guide gives the usual monthly units for 2 BHK, 3 BHK and larger homes, turns them into kW, panels, roof space, cost after subsidy and payback with the SolarSense calculator, and shows how the answer changes across 22 states.

Quick answer: a 2 BHK home typically uses about 150–300 units a month, which needs about 1–2 kW of rooftop solar in a state with 5.5 peak sun hours such as Delhi. A 3 BHK home typically uses about 300–600 units, which needs about 2–3.7 kW. At the calculator's benchmark price a typical 2 BHK system (250 units, 1.6 kW) costs about ₹67,500 after subsidy and a typical 3 BHK system (450 units, 2.8 kW) about ₹1.22 lakh, both paying back in under 5 years in Delhi. These sizes cover about 80% of your units; installers who quote 2–3 kW for a 2 BHK and 3–5 kW for a 3 BHK are sizing for 100% plus room to grow.

How much electricity does a 2 BHK or 3 BHK use?

Published 2026 sizing guides put typical consumption at roughly these ranges. Treat them as a starting point only: your own bill is always the better number.

The air conditioner is what moves a home from the bottom of its range to the top. A 1.5 ton AC run 8 hours a day uses roughly 190–265 units a month if it is a 5-star inverter model, 240–310 for a 3-star inverter and 360–430 for an older non-inverter or window AC; see solar for AC for the detail. So a 3 BHK with two ACs on summer nights can use twice the units of the same flat without them. Because AC use is seasonal, take the average of 12 monthly bills rather than a May or June bill, or the system will be sized for your worst month.

Up to about 450 units a month, payback stays between 4.6 and 5 years because the system is under 3 kW, where the PM Surya Ghar subsidy pays ₹30,000 a kW for the first 2 kW and ₹18,000 for the third. Above that the subsidy stops at ₹78,000, so bigger 3 BHK and 4 BHK systems pay back a little more slowly, about 5.3 years at 600 units and 5.9 years at 800. The saving is about two-thirds of the bill: the system makes about 78–80% of your units, and the calculator assumes you use 65% of them as they are generated and export the rest at the buyback rate. Why installers often quote a bigger system

Many sizing charts recommend 2–3 kW for a 2 BHK and 3–5 kW for a 3 BHK, larger than the table above. The difference is mostly what the system is sized to do:

  • 100% instead of about 80%. The calculator divides your daily units by peak sun hours, and panels deliver about 78% of their rating after losses, so its size covers about four-fifths of your use. To generate every unit, divide by sun hours × 0.78 instead: a 450-unit Delhi home needs 15 ÷ (5.5 × 0.78) = 3.5 kW rather than 2.8 kW, and a 250-unit home about 2 kW rather than 1.6 kW.
  • Growth. Adding an AC or an electric car later is easier if the system is already larger; an EV driven 40 km a day adds about 190 units a month (see solar for EV charging).
  • The subsidy cap. The subsidy is the same ₹78,000 from 3 kW upward, so 3 kW is the smallest size that collects the full subsidy.

Going larger is reasonable if you will use the units, but surplus units are usually exported at the buyback rate, which is about 30–60% of the tariff across the calculator's 22 states, so extra kW you do not use pay back slowly. The 3 kW vs 5 kW guide shows the payback of each size in nine states, including what happens when a home oversizes.

Flat, builder floor or independent house?

  • Independent house or villa: you own the roof and your electricity connection, so you can install a rooftop system and claim the household subsidy, up to ₹78,000.
  • Builder floor: the terrace is often shared or belongs to the top floor. Agree in writing who may use which part of the roof before booking an installer; each household needs its own connection and its own plant for the household subsidy.
  • Flat in an apartment block: the roof is usually common property, so solar generally goes in through the society or RWA to power common areas such as lifts, pumps and lighting, with ₹18,000 per kW of subsidy for that. Some states also allow group or virtual net metering that can share credits with individual flats. See the housing society solar guide.
  • Sanctioned load: national net-metering rules allow rooftop solar up to your sanctioned load, so a 3 BHK on a small single-phase connection may need a load enhancement first.

Frequently asked questions

How many kW of solar do I need for a 2 BHK?
A 2 BHK typically uses 150–300 units a month, which needs about 1–2 kW of rooftop solar at 5.5 peak sun hours, for example 1.6 kW in Delhi for 250 units. In less sunny states such as Kerala or West Bengal the same 250 units need about 1.9 kW. Choose about a quarter more if you want solar to cover all of your units or plan to add an AC.
How many kW of solar do I need for a 3 BHK?
A 3 BHK typically uses 300–600 units a month, which needs about 2–3.7 kW at 5.5 peak sun hours. For a typical 450 units the calculator sizes 2.8 kW in Delhi, 2.4 kW in Rajasthan and 3.4 kW in Kerala or West Bengal. Homes with two or three ACs in regular use are at the top of that range, and some choose 4–5 kW to cover all of their units.
What does solar for a 3 BHK cost after subsidy?
For a typical 450 units a month in Delhi, the calculator puts a 2.8 kW system at ₹1,96,115 before subsidy. The PM Surya Ghar subsidy at that size is ₹74,400, so the net cost is ₹1,21,715, saving about ₹2,100 a month with a payback of about 4.8 years. Across the 22 states the net cost is about ₹1.02–1.58 lakh and payback 4.4–9.3 years.
Can I install solar on my flat?
In most apartment blocks the roof is common property, so an individual flat owner usually cannot put up their own system. The society or RWA can install solar for common areas and gets ₹18,000 per kW under PM Surya Ghar. Where a resident has their own connection and roof space for their own plant, the household subsidy of up to ₹78,000 applies.
