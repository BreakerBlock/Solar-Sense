Solar Water Pump in India: PM-KUSUM Status, Subsidy, Size and Cost

A solar pump replaces the diesel set or the grid connection on a borewell, well or farm pond. This guide covers where the PM-KUSUM subsidy stands after its first phase ended in March 2026, how its subsidy split worked, a state scheme that is still installing pumps, what size of pump and panels you need, what a 3, 5, 7.5 or 10 HP solar pump costs in 2026, and how the numbers compare with diesel.

Quick answer (November 2026): PM-KUSUM's first phase ended on 31 March 2026; only projects already awarded are still being finished. PM-KUSUM 2.0 has been proposed (reported outlay up to about ₹51,000 crore, with an agrivoltaics component) but its terms still await government approval. Under phase 1, a standalone pump of up to 7.5 HP got 30% central + at least 30% state support, so the farmer paid 40% or less (50% central and 20% or less from the farmer in the North-East, hill states and islands). A solar pump needs roughly 1 kW of panels per HP, and 2026 price lists put a 5 HP system at about ₹2.8–4.5 lakh before subsidy.

PM-KUSUM in November 2026: where it stands

Phase 1 has ended. PM-KUSUM (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan) ran from 2019 to 31 March 2026. Officials said before the deadline that the existing scheme would not be formally extended, and reports since then describe the first phase as having ended in March 2026. An MNRE office memorandum of 28 March 2026 gave projects that already had a power purchase agreement or notice to proceed by 31 December 2025 more time to finish:

At the end of September 2026 MNRE extended the financial-closure deadline for eligible Component A and Component C (feeder-level) projects to 30 November 2026, while it finalises the details of PM-KUSUM 2.0. The 31 March 2027 completion date was reported as unchanged, and MNRE said it would not be extended further. Telangana's regulator has also applied the March 2027 Component A deadline to its state's projects.

PM-KUSUM 2.0 is proposed, not yet approved. Reports in October 2026 put the proposed financial requirement at up to about ₹51,000 crore, including liabilities from phase 1, with a dedicated agrivoltaics component (crops and panels on the same land) and more emphasis on feeder-level solarisation and battery storage. Its outlay, eligibility and subsidy rates remain subject to formal government approval, so nobody can yet tell you what subsidy a new pump will get under it. Treat any website or agent quoting fixed PM-KUSUM 2.0 subsidy figures with caution.

What to do now: if you have an application or allotment from phase 1, check its status with your state nodal agency (the state renewable energy agency or DISCOM that ran PM-KUSUM in your state). If you are starting fresh, check whether your state runs its own pump scheme (Maharashtra's is described below), or wait for the 2.0 guidelines. Only apply through the official state or national portal; fake PM-KUSUM registration sites asking for fees have been a recurring problem.

How the PM-KUSUM subsidy worked (phase 1)

For standalone pumps (Component B) and solarising grid pumps (Component C, individual pumps), the centre paid 30% of the MNRE benchmark cost or the tender-discovered cost, whichever was lower. The state paid at least 30%, and the farmer paid the rest, at most 40%. The farmer's share could usually be taken as a bank loan, leaving a small upfront payment. In the North-Eastern states, Sikkim, Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Lakshadweep and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands the central share was 50% and the farmer's share at most 20%.

The ₹3,50,000 is only an example within the 2026 price range for a 5 HP system; the subsidy was calculated on the benchmark or tender price, not on any quote you got. Other phase 1 rules worth knowing, since 2.0 may keep some of them:

  • Pump size: standalone pumps up to 7.5 HP were supported. Larger pumps were allowed, but the subsidy stayed at the 7.5 HP amount.
  • Groundwater: new pumps were not allowed in over-exploited ("dark") groundwater zones; converting an existing diesel pump there was allowed if the farmer used micro-irrigation.
  • Grid pumps (Component C): a farmer could add solar panels of up to twice the pump's kW and sell the surplus to the DISCOM, so the pump earns money in the months it is idle.
  • Feeder-level solarisation: central support of 30% of cost, capped at ₹1.05 crore per MW (30% of a ₹3.5 crore per MW benchmark), for solar plants that feed a whole agricultural feeder.

A state pump scheme that is still installing: Maharashtra

Maharashtra's Magel Tyala Saur Krushi Pump Yojana, run by MSEDCL, supplies a complete off-grid solar pump set to farmers for 10% of the cost (5% for Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe farmers); the central and state governments pay the rest. Pump size follows land holding: up to 3 HP for up to 2.5 acres, 5 HP for 2.51–5 acres and 7.5 HP above 5 acres (a smaller pump can be chosen). You need an assured water source such as a well, borewell, farm pond or perennial stream, and must not have had a pump under the earlier Atal or Mukhyamantri solar pump schemes. The set comes with a five-year repair guarantee including insurance. MSEDCL placed an order for 10,000 more off-grid pumps under the scheme in September 2026.

On the ₹3,50,000 example above, 10% is ₹35,000 and 5% is ₹17,500. Other states have run their own pump schemes in the past; ask your state renewable energy agency what is open, and check the details on an official site before paying anything.

What size solar pump do you need?

Pump size depends on how much water you need a day and how deep you lift it (the "head": water level plus pipe losses). A submersible pump in a deep borewell needs more power for the same water than a surface pump lifting from a pond. The installer should give you the litres per day the pump delivers at your head; compare that with what your crops need in the driest month. The panels are then sized to the pump, at roughly 1 kW of panels per HP, so the pump runs for most of a sunny day:

*At 5 peak sun hours with the calculator's 78% allowance for losses (about 3.9 units per kW a day); see solar generation per kW by state. Panel area is the panels alone; a ground mount with walkways and row gaps takes more land. 1 HP = 0.746 kW.

Two choices matter besides size. AC or DC pump: DC pumps (usually brushless) are more efficient at low sunlight and suit smaller sizes, but cost more and are harder to repair locally; AC pumps use a standard motor with a solar pump controller (VFD) and are the norm at 5 HP and above. Submersible or surface: submersibles go inside a borewell and lift from depth; surface pumps are cheaper but only lift a few metres of suction from a pond, tank or shallow well.

Without any subsidy, a solar pump still costs far more upfront than a diesel set. Its case rests on fuel: the sun is free, and there is no diesel to buy or carry to the field.

Solar pump vs diesel pump: the running cost

Work out what your diesel pump costs you now: litres per hour × hours a year × price per litre. To find litres per hour, fill the tank, run the pump for an hour and measure what it took. As an example, a pumpset that burns 1 litre an hour, run 4 hours a day for 150 days a year, uses 600 litres; at about ₹95 a litre (Delhi, November 2026) that is ₹57,000 a year on fuel, before oil changes and repairs.

  • Paying full price for a 5 HP solar pump (₹2.8–4.5 lakh), that fuel saving alone repays it in about 5–8 years; panels last 25 years.
  • Paying a 40% share (about ₹1.1–1.8 lakh), it is about 2–3 years.
  • Paying a 10% share under a state scheme (about ₹28,000–45,000), it is less than a year.

The trade-off: a solar pump only runs in daylight and gives less water on cloudy days and in the monsoon, while a diesel set runs whenever you start it. Most farmers handle this by pumping during the day into a tank or farm pond, and by using drip or sprinkler irrigation so the daytime water goes further.

Already on a grid connection? Solarising your pump

If your pump runs on an agricultural grid connection, the question is different: power to farm pumps is usually free or heavily subsidised, so solar saves you little on the bill. What solarising does is let you sell the surplus. Under PM-KUSUM Component C, panels of up to twice the pump's kW could be added and the extra units sold to the DISCOM, which turns the months the pump sits idle into income. Feeder-level solarisation, now the focus of the 2.0 proposal, does this for a whole feeder at once and gives farmers reliable daytime power. Whether a grid pump can be solarised now depends on your state's scheme and on PM-KUSUM 2.0; the buyback rates by state give a sense of what exported units earn.

Before you buy a solar pump

  • Ask for the litres per day at your head, not just the HP, and for the pump curve.
  • Check the pump, controller and panels against MNRE's specifications for solar pumps; a scheme pump must meet them anyway.
  • Get the warranty in writing: scheme pumps came with five years of repair and maintenance; panels should carry a long performance warranty.
  • Look for remote monitoring, which phase 1 required on scheme pumps and which makes warranty claims easier.
  • Plan theft protection for the panels and a fenced mounting structure strong enough for your area's wind.
  • Apply only through your state nodal agency's official portal; never pay a "registration fee" to a website or caller.

Frequently asked questions

Is the PM-KUSUM solar pump scheme still open in 2026?
Not for new pumps under the original terms. PM-KUSUM's first phase ended on 31 March 2026, and only projects awarded by 31 December 2025 are being finished, with pumps to be commissioned by 30 September 2026. PM-KUSUM 2.0 has been proposed, with a reported outlay of up to about ₹51,000 crore and an agrivoltaics component, but its subsidy rates and eligibility still await government approval as of November 2026. Some states, such as Maharashtra, run their own solar pump schemes.
How much subsidy is there on a solar water pump?
Under PM-KUSUM phase 1 the centre paid 30% of the benchmark or tender cost, the state at least 30%, and the farmer at most 40%; in the North-East, hill states and islands the centre paid 50% and the farmer at most 20%. Maharashtra's Magel Tyala Saur Krushi Pump Yojana charges farmers 10% of the cost (5% for SC/ST farmers). Subsidy rates under PM-KUSUM 2.0 are not yet known.
What size solar panel system does a 5 HP pump need?
About 5 kW of panels, at roughly 1 kW per HP, which takes around 275 sq ft of panel area and makes about 20 units on an average sunny day. A 3 HP pump needs about 3 kW and a 7.5 HP pump about 7.5 kW. The right pump size depends on the water you need a day and how deep you lift it.
What is the price of a 5 HP solar pump in India?
About ₹2.8–4.5 lakh for a complete system before subsidy in 2026 price lists, with submersible and DC pumps towards the top. A 3 HP system is about ₹1.5–2.8 lakh, 7.5 HP about ₹4.0–5.2 lakh and 10 HP about ₹4.5–7.3 lakh.
Can a solar pump run at night or on cloudy days?
Solar pumps normally have no battery, so they run only in daylight and give less water on cloudy days. Farmers usually pump during the day into a tank or farm pond, and use drip or sprinkler irrigation so the water goes further. Some pumps can also run from the grid or a generator as a backup.
Is a solar pump cheaper than a diesel pump?
Over its life, usually yes. A diesel pumpset that burns 1 litre an hour for 600 hours a year costs about ₹57,000 a year in fuel at ₹95 a litre. At that rate a full-price 5 HP solar pump repays itself in about 5–8 years, a 40% share in about 2–3 years and a 10% state-scheme share in under a year, while the panels last about 25 years.
