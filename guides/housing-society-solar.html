Rooftop Solar for Housing Societies and RWAs: Subsidy, Size, Cost and Payback

Lifts, water pumps, corridor and parking lights make up a society's common-area bill, and a shared roof can often cover a large part of it. This guide explains the PM Surya Ghar subsidy for Group Housing Societies and Resident Welfare Associations, how to size a common-area system from the bill, what it costs in 2026, and how fast it pays back.

Quick answer: a housing society or RWA gets a central subsidy of ₹18,000 per kW for solar that powers common facilities, including EV charging, up to 500 kW, and no more than 3 kW per house. Size the system from the common-area connection's monthly units: in Delhi, about 8,250 units a month points to 50 kW. Using the calculator's commercial cost benchmark, a 50 kW system costs about ₹23.3 lakh, the subsidy is ₹9 lakh, and at a ₹7 tariff it pays back in roughly 3–4 years.

How the housing society subsidy works

Under PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana, Group Housing Societies (GHS) and Resident Welfare Associations (RWA) have their own subsidy rate, separate from the household slabs:

  • ₹18,000 per kW of solar capacity serving common facilities, which explicitly includes EV charging.
  • Up to 500 kW per society, worked out at 3 kW per house. A society with 40 homes can claim on at most 120 kW.
  • The ceiling is inclusive of rooftop plants installed by individual residents of the society. If some residents already have their own subsidised systems, their capacity counts towards the same limit.
  • The system is grid-connected on the common-area connection, installed by a vendor registered on the national portal, and must use domestically made (DCR) solar modules to qualify, as with the household subsidy.

Unlike the household subsidy, which stops at ₹78,000, the society rate keeps applying per kW up to the ceiling, so a larger common-area system still gets meaningful support. See the PM Surya Ghar subsidy guide for the household slabs and the national portal.

These are ceilings, not targets. Most societies' common-area use justifies far less than 3 kW per home, and the size should come from the bill, as below. In a society of 80 homes where ten residents have already installed 3 kW each, the ceiling of 240 kW falls to 210 kW for the common-area plant.

What size system does the society need?

A common-area connection typically powers passenger and service lifts, water pumps (overhead tank, borewell, STP), corridor, staircase and compound lighting, basement ventilation, security systems and gates, and sometimes a clubhouse, gym or shared EV chargers. Loads billed to individual flats or shops are not part of it.

Size it the same way the solar calculator sizes a home: average monthly units on the common-area bill ÷ 30 ÷ peak sun hours. Use units (kWh), not the rupee amount, averaged over 12 months. In a less sunny state the same system makes less, so it covers fewer units: at the Indian average of 5 sun hours, 50 kW suits about 7,500 units a month and produces about 71,175 units a year. State-by-state output per kW is in how many units 1 kW of solar generates. As with homes, a system sized this way covers roughly 80% of a year's use, because of the 0.78 performance ratio for real-world losses.

When the roof is the limit

In high-rise societies the roof, not the bill, often sets the size. Water tanks, lift machine rooms, stair heads and shade from neighbouring towers cut the usable area. Elevated mounting structures can put panels above tanks and walkways, and higher-efficiency TOPCon panels fit more kW per square foot (see solar panel types compared), but both add cost. Several towers can each carry part of the array if they are on the same common-area connection.

What 2026 quotes look like

Published 2026 prices for society-sized systems vary widely: worked examples for about 50 kW range from roughly ₹38,000 to ₹62,000 per kW before subsidy. The higher end usually reflects DCR panels (needed for the subsidy and pricier than imported ones), elevated structures over tanks and walkways, long cable runs down a tall building, and lift-and-crane logistics. The calculator's ₹46,513 per kW at 50 kW sits in the middle. Compare quotes line by line with the checklist in the solar panel cost guide, and do not compare a DCR quote with a non-DCR one.

Can the savings reach individual flats?

On a standard net-metering setup, the society's solar offsets the common-area bill only: units are used by the lifts, pumps and lights first, and any surplus is exported and credited to that same connection, as explained in net metering explained. Residents benefit through lower maintenance charges, not on their own bills.

Some states go further with group net metering or virtual net metering, which let solar credits from one roof be shared across several connections, including individual flats. Delhi and Maharashtra, for example, have such provisions, while others do not. The rules, eligible consumers and charges differ by state and change through regulatory amendments, so ask your DISCOM what is currently available before deciding on a larger plant meant to cover flats.

How a society applies

  1. Committee resolution. The managing committee or general body approves the project and names an authorised signatory.
  2. Collect 12 months of common-area bills and work out average monthly units, then check roof space and the sanctioned load of the common-area connection.
  3. Register on the national portal (pmsuryaghar.gov.in) as a GHS/RWA, with the society's registration certificate and the common-area consumer number.
  4. DISCOM feasibility approval for the planned capacity.
  5. Choose a vendor registered on the portal and get comparable quotes with DCR modules.
  6. Installation and net meter. After commissioning, the DISCOM inspects the plant and installs a bi-directional meter on the common-area connection.
  7. Subsidy is credited to the society's bank account after the inspection report is uploaded.

A society can fund the plant from its sinking fund, a one-time contribution, or a loan. The collateral-free PM Surya Ghar loans described in the solar loan and EMI guide are for household systems up to 3 kW, so a society should ask banks about loans for GHS/RWA projects separately.

Checklist before the society signs

  • The vendor is registered on the national portal and the quote names DCR modules, inverter make and capacity, and structure type.
  • The size comes from 12 months of common-area units and fits within the society's ceiling (3 kW per house, minus residents' own plants, maximum 500 kW).
  • A structural check confirms the roof can carry the panels and structure, and the waterproofing is protected.
  • Generation is guaranteed in writing, with remote monitoring the committee can see.
  • Cleaning, maintenance and inverter service are covered for at least 5 years, with clear response times.
  • The vendor handles the portal, DISCOM paperwork and net meter, and the subsidy goes to the society's account.

Frequently asked questions

How much solar subsidy does a housing society get?
Under PM Surya Ghar, a Group Housing Society or RWA gets ₹18,000 per kW for solar that powers common facilities, including EV charging, up to 500 kW, limited to 3 kW per house. The limit includes rooftop plants installed by individual residents. A 50 kW common-area system therefore gets ₹9 lakh.
How much does a 50 kW solar system cost for a housing society?
Published 2026 examples range from about ₹38,000 to ₹62,000 per kW before subsidy, so roughly ₹19–31 lakh for 50 kW. The calculator's commercial benchmark gives about ₹23.26 lakh; after the ₹9 lakh society subsidy the net cost is about ₹14.26 lakh, and at a ₹7 tariff in Delhi it pays back in about 3.2 years.
What size solar system does our society need?
Divide the common-area connection's average monthly units by 30 and by your state's peak sun hours. In Delhi (5.5 hours), 8,250 units a month points to 50 kW and 3,300 units to 20 kW. Plan about 80–100 sq ft of shadow-free roof per kW, so 50 kW needs about 4,000–5,000 sq ft.
Can residents of a housing society also get the household solar subsidy?
A resident with their own electricity connection and their own rooftop plant can apply for the household subsidy, up to ₹78,000. In apartment buildings the roof is usually common property, so this is more common in societies of independent houses. Any such plants count towards the society's 500 kW (3 kW per house) ceiling.
