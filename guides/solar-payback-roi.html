Solar Payback Period and ROI in India (2026): Is Rooftop Solar Worth It?

Whether rooftop solar is worth it comes down to three numbers: what you pay after the PM Surya Ghar subsidy, what the panels save you each year, and how long they keep doing it. This guide works through all three with the solar payback calculator's own figures, shows how payback changes with your monthly units and your state, and lists the cases where solar is not a good buy.

Quick answer: for a home on a grid connection, rooftop solar usually pays for itself in about 4.5 to 9.5 years, depending mostly on your state's tariff. A Delhi home using 300 units a month pays ₹78,650 after subsidy for a 1.9 kW system that saves about ₹17,283 a year: a 4.6-year payback, or a simple return of about 22% a year. Panels are warranted for around 25 years, so the system keeps saving for roughly 20 years after it has paid for itself.

How solar payback and ROI are worked out

The calculator uses the simplest honest method:

  • Net cost = the full system price (panels, inverter, structure, wiring, installation and the net meter) minus the central PM Surya Ghar subsidy: ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW, ₹18,000 for the third kW, and ₹78,000 for any home system of 3 kW or more (see the subsidy guide).
  • First-year benefit = units you use yourself × your tariff + units you export × your DISCOM's buyback rate. The calculator assumes you use 65% of what the panels make, which is cautious for a family that is home in the daytime.
  • Payback period = net cost ÷ first-year benefit.
  • Simple yearly return (ROI) = first-year benefit ÷ net cost. A 5-year payback is a 20% simple return; a 10-year payback is 10%.

This is the same arithmetic you would use to compare solar with a fixed deposit or any other use of the money: put your own deposit or loan rate next to the yearly return. The return from solar comes as a smaller electricity bill rather than as interest.

Why payback is fastest at around 300–400 units. The subsidy is ₹30,000 per kW only for the first 2 kW, then ₹18,000 for the third and nothing beyond 3 kW. The biggest lever: how much solar you use yourself

A unit you use yourself saves your full tariff; a unit you export earns only the buyback rate, often about half. For the Delhi 1.9 kW system (about 2,993 units a year), the same panels pay back at very different speeds depending on when you use power:

Share of solar you use yourselfFirst-year benefitPayback
40% (out all day, most use at night)₹14,6655.4 yrs
65% (the calculator's assumption)₹17,2834.6 yrs
100% (all daytime use, or exports credited at the full tariff)₹20,9513.8 yrs

The 40% and 100% rows apply the calculator's own method with a different self-use share. Running the washing machine, water pump, geyser or AC in the daytime moves you down this table. Under net metering, exports that offset your own use within a billing cycle are often credited at close to your full tariff, which is why the calculator's 65% assumption tends to understate the saving.

  • Panel degradation. The calculator uses 0.55% a year for Mono PERC panels (0.4% for TOPCon), so after 25 years the panels still make about 88% of their first-year output. See solar panel types for each technology's rate and warranty.
  • Tariff rises. Electricity tariffs have tended to rise over time, and each rise makes the units you no longer buy worth more. The 3% a year in the last row is the escalation the calculator uses for its surplus-space cash flow; it is an assumption, not a forecast. Home results in the calculator stay at today's tariff, which is the cautious choice.
  • Inverter replacement. Panels are typically warranted for 25 years, but inverter warranties are much shorter (the Delhi solar portal quotes 5 to 7 years), so plan for at least one inverter replacement during the system's life. In the calculator's cost breakdown the inverter for this 1.9 kW system is about ₹22,000 at today's price, or roughly 1.3 years of its savings. Our solar inverter size guide explains how to size the replacement.
  • Maintenance. Under PM Surya Ghar, the registered vendor must provide five years of comprehensive operation and maintenance, and the complete system is warranted for five years from commissioning (the scheme's model consumer–vendor agreement). After that, regular panel cleaning is the main running cost.

When rooftop solar is not worth it

  • Your bill is already very low or free. Solar saves only what you would otherwise pay. If a state scheme already makes your first block of units free, or your bill is only a few hundred rupees, there is little left to offset.
  • The roof is shaded or not yours to keep. Trees, water tanks or a taller building next door can cut output sharply. If you rent, or expect to move within a few years, you may not stay long enough to reach payback. In a flat, look at solar for housing societies instead.
  • You oversize far beyond your use. Extra units are exported at the buyback rate, so kW you do not need return much less than the first few. If you want to use a large spare roof or plot as an investment, read earning from surplus space first.
  • You go off-grid where the grid works. Off-grid systems get no subsidy and need a battery bank: a Delhi home using 300 units a month pays ₹6,32,368 for an off-grid system that takes about 14 years to pay back even counting the diesel it replaces, against 4.6 years on-grid. See the off-grid solar guide; with frequent power cuts, a hybrid system with a small battery is usually the better middle way.
  • You borrow at a high rate. The PM Surya Ghar loan for systems up to 3 kW is linked to the RBI repo rate; larger loans cost more. Compare the EMI with the monthly saving in our solar loan and EMI guide.

For a shop, office or factory the subsidy does not apply but the tariff is usually higher and there is a depreciation benefit: see commercial rooftop solar.

Frequently asked questions

What is the payback period of rooftop solar in India?
For a home on a grid connection, about 4.5 to 9.5 years in the solar calculator, depending mainly on the state's tariff. A home using 300 units a month pays back in about 4.4 years in Rajasthan, 4.6 years in Delhi, 5.0 years in Maharashtra, 7.2 years in Tamil Nadu and 8.8 years in Goa. Off-grid systems with batteries take much longer, about 14 years.
What is the ROI of a 3 kW solar system?
In Delhi, a 3 kW on-grid system costs ₹2,09,552 in the calculator, or ₹1,31,552 after the ₹78,000 subsidy, and saves about ₹27,195 in the first year. That is a simple return of about 20.7% a year and a payback of about 4.8 years. In states with lower tariffs the return is lower: about 10.7% a year in Goa, where the same system pays back in about 9.3 years.
Is solar worth it for a home using 100 units a month?
Usually yes, if you pay for those units. In Delhi a 0.7 kW system costs ₹34,029 after subsidy, saves about ₹6,325 a year and pays back in about 5.4 years. It is not worth it if your bill is already zero or close to zero under a state free-power scheme, because solar can only save what you would otherwise pay.
Does the solar payback include panel degradation and inverter replacement?
No. The calculator's payback is net cost ÷ first-year benefit, at today's tariff. Panel output falling about 0.55% a year lengthens it slightly; tariff rises shorten it. An inverter replacement, likely once in 25 years because inverter warranties are much shorter than panel warranties, costs about ₹22,000 for a 1.9 kW system at today's prices.
How can I make my solar system pay back faster?
Use more of the solar yourself: run the washing machine, pump, geyser or AC in the daytime, because a unit you use saves your full tariff while an exported unit earns only the buyback rate. For a Delhi 1.9 kW system, moving from 65% to 100% self-use shortens payback from about 4.6 to 3.8 years. Size the system to your own use, and get at least three quotes.
