Earning From Surplus Space: Sell Solar Power From Your Roof or Land

If you have more space than you need power — a big terrace, a warehouse roof, a shed, or a small idle plot — you can fill it with solar and sell what you don't use. Here's how the economics actually work, and the constraint that trips most people up.

Best for: Homeowners with large terraces, small businesses with warehouse roofs, or anyone sitting on an idle plot near an existing electricity connection.
Quick answer: a Delhi home using 300 units a month that fills 552 sq ft of panels (10 kW) instead of 276 sq ft (5 kW) earns more in total — about ₹67,400 a year instead of ₹38,300 in the calculator — but the return on each rupee falls from 17.5% to 13.3% and payback stretches from 5.7 to 7.5 years, because almost everything extra is exported at the ₹3.50 buyback rate rather than saving your ₹7 tariff. The PM Surya Ghar subsidy stays at ₹78,000 whatever the size, and most DISCOMs won't connect more solar than your sanctioned load. Check that first.

The core idea

Your own consumption might need 3 kWp. But your roof has room for 12 kWp. Those extra 9 kWp generate power you'll never use yourself — so it goes to the grid, and the DISCOM pays you for it. Instead of sizing solar to your bill, you size it to your space.

What you actually earn

Self-consumed units are worth your full retail tariff (₹5–7.5/kWh) because you avoid buying them. Exported units earn the buyback rate (₹2–3.5/kWh), which is always lower. So the economics are strongest when you use as much as you can and sell the remainder.

The single highest-return move is shifting load into daylight hours — charging an EV during the day, running the washing machine and dishwasher at noon, pre-cooling the house before evening, heating water electrically. Every unit you shift from "export at ₹2.50" to "self-use at ₹7" is worth far more than adding another panel.

How much solar fits on your roof or land

Mono PERC panels cover about 55 sq ft per kW (about 50 sq ft with TOPCon). On a real terrace you also need walkways, edge clearance and gaps so rows don't shade each other, so plan for 80–100 sq ft of shadow-free roof per kW: a 1,000 sq ft terrace holds roughly 10–12 kW, and 1,500 sq ft roughly 15–19 kW. On the ground, 2026 project guides put a fixed-tilt plant at 4–5 acres per MW, or about 200–250 kW per acre, so a quarter-acre plot fits roughly 50–60 kW. Using the calculator: the surplus-space mode converts the area you enter into kW at the panel's own footprint (about 55 sq ft per kW for Mono PERC, × 1.4 for ground mounts). Enter the area the panels themselves will cover, not your whole terrace. For land, treat its kW figure as an upper bound: at 1.4 × panel area it puts about 140 kW on a quarter acre, well above the 50–60 kW that row spacing allows in practice. Its cost per kW, payback and yield are still a fair guide; scale the kW down to 200–250 per acre.

The three real constraints

1. Sanctioned load cap. Under the national Electricity (Rights of Consumers) Rules, net metering is allowed up to 500 kW or your sanctioned load, whichever is lower, and most state regulations follow that; Bihar's October 2026 draft regulations, for example, set exactly this limit. A few DISCOMs are stricter: in February 2026 Maharashtra's MSEDCL began linking domestic rooftop capacity to the past 12 months' consumption, a change rooftop installers contested. So if your connection is 5 kW, expect to need a load enhancement before you can install 15 kWp. Since 2024, systems up to 10 kW no longer need a technical feasibility study, but that doesn't lift the sanctioned-load limit. Check this before anything else — it kills more projects than cost does. In the calculator, keep the export cap at 100% unless your DISCOM confirms a higher limit in writing.

2. Subsidy ceiling. PM Surya Ghar pays ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW and ₹18,000 for the third, capped at ₹78,000 for any system of 3 kW or more; a 5 kW, 10 kW or 15 kW home system gets the same ₹78,000. See the subsidy guide for the slabs.

3. Buyback isn't cash everywhere. Some DISCOMs credit units against future bills rather than paying cash. If you export far more than you consume, those credits may expire unused at the financial year end. Where surplus is paid, it is usually settled once a year at a rate the state commission sets, often the DISCOM's average power purchase cost: Kerala's regulator upheld ₹3.26 a unit (for FY2023-24) in July 2026, and Bihar's October 2026 draft proposes 50% of the commission's feed-in tariff. That is why the calculator values every exported unit at the buyback rate, not your tariff. More in net metering explained.

Small ground mounts on idle land

A plot changes the arithmetic in two ways. Ground-mounted plants are cheaper per kW: 2026 project guides quote about ₹35,000–45,000 per kW turnkey, excluding land and grid connection, for plants around 1 MW, and the calculator uses ₹50,000 per kW for small ground mounts, before scale discounts. But a plot rarely has a large electricity load of its own, so almost everything is exported, and net metering is still limited to the sanctioned load of the connection. Selling most of a plant's output usually needs a separate arrangement with the DISCOM, such as gross metering at a feed-in tariff, or a power purchase agreement with a buyer.

Agricultural land usually has to be converted to non-agricultural use first; one October 2026 project guide budgets 3–6 months and ₹2–5 lakh for conversion in states that require it. If you'd rather not run the plant yourself, developers lease private land for solar; a May 2026 guide put typical rents at ₹15,000–50,000 per acre per year on 25–30 year leases with 3–5% escalation.

In the calculator's ground-mount mode (Rajasthan, ₹2.85 buyback, almost all output exported, 40% accelerated depreciation at a 30% tax rate), a plot pays back in about 8 years with a 12.6–12.9% first-year yield. That return depends on the depreciation, which needs business income to use, and on actually being paid for the exports.

Realistic returns

A 10 kWp rooftop in Delhi (₹7/kWh tariff), for a home using about 600 units a month that self-uses 47% of the output, returns about ₹80,600 in the first year on ₹5.07 lakh after subsidy: a 15.9% yield and a 6.3-year payback in the calculator. Push self-consumption higher and it improves sharply; export most of it and it falls towards 10–13%. The yield also rises every year as grid tariffs climb ~3% annually while your cost stays fixed — and because avoided electricity bills aren't income, that part of the return is effectively tax-free (cash paid for exports may be treated differently; ask a tax advisor). Frequently asked questions

How do I find my sanctioned load?
It's printed on your electricity bill, usually in kW. Under the national rules this number (up to 500 kW) is normally the largest system your DISCOM will approve under net metering without a connection upgrade. A few DISCOMs set their own limits, so confirm with yours.
Is this the same as a solar power plant?
No. This is about filling spare space you already have — a terrace, a shed roof, a small plot — not building a utility-scale plant with land acquisition, PPAs, and grid connectivity engineering. It stays close to a normal rooftop installation, just larger.
Can I claim depreciation benefits?
If the system is on a commercial connection and you have business income, you can typically claim 40% accelerated depreciation in year one, which is a meaningful tax benefit. This does not apply to residential systems. Consult a tax advisor for your situation.
Can I install more solar than my sanctioned load?
Usually not under net metering. The national rules allow net metering up to 500 kW or your sanctioned load, whichever is lower, and most states follow that. To install more, apply to your DISCOM for a load enhancement first, which typically means a fee and possibly a meter or cable upgrade.
Do I get a bigger subsidy on a 10 kW home system?
No. The PM Surya Ghar central subsidy is ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW and ₹18,000 for the third, capped at ₹78,000 for any system of 3 kW or more. A 10 kW home system gets ₹78,000, the same as a 3 kW one.
How much can I earn by selling solar power to the grid?
Exported units earn your DISCOM's buyback rate, about ₹2–3.50 a unit, not your tariff. In the calculator, a 10 kW Delhi rooftop for a home using 300 units a month exports about 12,000 units a year worth about ₹42,200, plus about ₹25,200 of bill savings on the units the home uses itself: ₹67,400 a year on ₹5.07 lakh after subsidy, a 7.5-year payback.
How many kW of solar fit on one acre of land?
About 200–250 kW on a fixed-tilt ground mount, since 2026 project guides put a 1 MW plant at 4–5 acres. On a rooftop, plan 80–100 sq ft of shadow-free roof per kW including walkways, or about 55 sq ft per kW of panel area alone with Mono PERC.
