Commercial Rooftop Solar in India: Sizing, Cost & Payback

Shops, offices, clinics, schools, warehouses and small factories use most of their electricity in daylight, on a higher tariff than homes. That makes rooftop solar work very differently for a business. Here is how to size a commercial system, what it costs, how the tax benefit replaces the home subsidy, and what payback to expect.

Quick answer: size a commercial system to the electricity you use during the day, not to your roof. At 5.5 peak sun hours, about 1 kW for every 165 units a month. A business using 6,600 units a month needs about 40 kW and 3,200–4,000 sq ft of shadow-free roof. There is no PM Surya Ghar subsidy for commercial connections, but 40% accelerated depreciation usually brings payback to 3–5 years when most of the output is used on site. Small shops and offices usually need 3–10 kW: see solar for shops and small offices.

How commercial solar differs from home solar

  • You use power while the sun shines. A home uses much of its electricity in the evening, so a lot of its solar output is exported at the low buyback rate. A shop or office open through the day can often use nearly all of its solar output directly, and each of those units is worth the full commercial tariff.
  • No residential subsidy. The PM Surya Ghar subsidy is only for residential connections. A commercial or industrial connection gets ₹0, but the business can claim accelerated depreciation instead (below). Housing societies and RWAs are the exception: their common-area solar gets ₹18,000 per kW, covered in solar for housing societies.
  • Time-of-day tariffs. Under the national consumer rules, commercial and industrial consumers with a maximum demand above 10 kW were to move to time-of-day (ToD) tariffs from 1 April 2024. The energy charge during the eight "solar hours" set by your state commission must be at least 20% below the normal rate, and at peak hours at least 20% above it. Solar replaces the cheaper solar-hours units, so value it at that rate.
  • Bigger systems, lower cost per kW. Fixed costs such as design, the net meter and the DISCOM application are spread over more kW.

Step 1: Size to your daytime use

Start from your monthly units (kWh) on the bill, averaged over 12 months. Then use the same rule as the calculator:

System size (kW) = monthly units ÷ 30 ÷ peak sun hours

Sized this way, a system produces about 78% of your units over a year, after real-world losses from heat, dust and the inverter. For a business that uses most of its power in the day, that is close to the sweet spot: almost every unit generated is used on site. If a large part of your use is at night (a cold store, a 24-hour hotel or a night shift), size down to your daytime load, because night-time units cannot come from the panels without batteries. Check your sanctioned load too: net metering stops there (Step 3).

Step 2: What it costs, and the tax benefit

For commercial roofs, the calculator starts from a benchmark of ₹52,000 per kW, then takes 6% off from 25 kW, a further 5% from 50 kW and a further 4% from 100 kW. With Mono PERC panels and the net meter, that works out to about ₹52,500 per kW at 20 kW, ₹49,000 at 40 kW and ₹44,500 at 100 kW. Real quotes depend on the roof structure, the panel and inverter brands, and the distance to your meter, so get two or three quotes.

Accelerated depreciation. Solar power generating systems used in a business are in the 40% depreciation block for renewable energy devices. From 1 April 2026 this is under Section 34 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, which replaced Section 32 of the 1961 Act with the same rate and written-down-value method. If the system is in use for fewer than 180 days in the financial year it is installed, only half the rate (20%) can be claimed that year. Commissioning by the end of September keeps the full 40%.

The calculator shows the year-one benefit as 40% of the system cost × a 30% tax rate, about 12% of the cost, and subtracts it from the price. Your real saving depends on your tax rate and regime, and on whether you have enough taxable profit to use it, so confirm it with your chartered accountant.

Domestic-content panels are not required. The DCR (domestically manufactured cells and modules) rule is a condition of the PM Surya Ghar subsidy. A commercial system that does not take the subsidy is free to use any panel that meets the standard technical and quality requirements.

Step 3: Net metering limits for businesses

Under the Electricity (Rights of Consumers) Rules, net metering is available up to 500 kW or your sanctioned load, whichever is lower. Above 500 kW, systems generally move to gross metering, where all generation is sold to the DISCOM at a fixed rate. Your state's regulations set the details: some cap total solar on a distribution transformer, some use net billing instead of unit-for-unit credit, and the national rules allow commissions to apply ToD tariffs to rooftop solar prosumers. Since a 2024 amendment, systems up to 10 kW need no technical feasibility study; larger ones get the study within 15 days. Our net metering guide covers the rules in detail.

Your sanctioned load or contract demand is on the bill. If the solar system you want is bigger, the DISCOM application for the higher load usually has to come first.

Solar for shops and small offices (3–10 kW)

Most shops, clinics, salons, coaching centres and small offices use a few hundred to about 1,700 units a month, so they need a 3–10 kW system rather than the 20–100 kW sizes above. The same rules apply (no PM Surya Ghar subsidy on a commercial connection, 40% accelerated depreciation instead), but a few things work differently at this size:

  • Your sanctioned load usually sets the size. Small commercial connections are often sanctioned at 3–10 kW, and net metering stops at the sanctioned load. If your bill says 5 kW, plan for 5 kW or apply to raise the load first.
  • Simpler approval. Systems up to 10 kW need no technical feasibility study from the DISCOM (Step 3).
  • Usually no time-of-day tariff. The national ToD rule covers commercial consumers with a maximum demand above 10 kW, so a small shop is normally billed at its full per-unit rate, and each unit of solar it uses saves that full rate.
  • Cost per kW is higher. Fixed costs such as the net meter are spread over fewer kW: in the calculator, about ₹54,400 per kW at 3 kW and ₹52,500 at 10 kW, against ₹44,500 at 100 kW.
  • Days closed matter. A shop shut one day a week cannot use that day's output; it is exported at the buyback rate. Opening hours that end at sunset suit solar best.

Size it the same way: at 5.5 peak sun hours, 3 kW suits about 500 units a month, 5 kW about 825 and 10 kW about 1,650. Roof needed is roughly 80–100 sq ft per kW, so 240–300 sq ft for 3 kW and 800–1,000 sq ft for 10 kW. Things to check first for a shop or office: who owns the roof (in a market complex or a rented unit you need the building owner's or association's written permission, and often a share of a common terrace); which connection the system will be on (the subsidy and the net meter follow the connection on your bill, and a shop on its own commercial meter gets no PM Surya Ghar subsidy); and whether the business has taxable income to use the depreciation. Without it, use the cost before depreciation: the 5 kW payback at ₹10 then becomes about 3.7 years.

Before you sign: a checklist for businesses

  • Own the roof, or have a long lease. The system pays for itself over years. If you rent, get the landlord's written consent and a lease that runs past the payback.
  • Check the roof's load capacity. Metal sheds and older RCC roofs may need a structural check before mounting rows of panels.
  • Look at 12 months of bills, including your ToD solar-hours rate, the fixed or demand charges (solar does not reduce these much) and your sanctioned load.
  • Ask about generation guarantees and who files the net metering application with the DISCOM.
  • Plan cleaning. Dust on industrial roofs can cut output noticeably; budget for regular washing.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a subsidy for commercial rooftop solar in India?
Not under PM Surya Ghar, which is only for residential connections. Commercial and industrial users instead claim 40% accelerated depreciation on the system in the first year, which cuts the business's income tax. The calculator shows this as about 12% of the system cost, assuming a 30% tax rate.
How much does a 50 kW commercial solar system cost?
Our calculator's benchmark for a 50 kW commercial rooftop with Mono PERC panels is about ₹23 lakh (roughly ₹46,500 per kW), before the depreciation benefit of about ₹2.8 lakh in year one. Actual quotes vary with the roof structure and equipment, so compare at least two or three.
What is the maximum solar system size a business can install with net metering?
Under the national consumer rules, net metering is allowed up to 500 kW or the sanctioned load, whichever is lower. Above 500 kW, gross metering generally applies. State regulations can add limits, such as transformer capacity, so check with your DISCOM.
What is the payback period for commercial rooftop solar?
In our calculator, a business in Maharashtra paying ₹10 a unit and using 90% of its solar output on site pays back a 20–100 kW system in about 2.7–3.2 years after the depreciation benefit. At ₹8 a unit it is 3.3–3.9 years, and using only half the output on site stretches a 40 kW system to 4.2–5.0 years.
What size solar system does a small shop or office need?
At 5.5 peak sun hours, about 1 kW for every 165 units a month: 3 kW suits a shop using about 500 units a month, 5 kW about 825 and 10 kW about 1,650. Net metering stops at your sanctioned load, so a shop with a 5 kW connection should plan for 5 kW or raise its load first. In our calculator, a 5 kW system for a Maharashtra shop costs about ₹2.67 lakh and pays back in about 3.2 years at ₹10 a unit.
Can a shop get the PM Surya Ghar solar subsidy?
Not on a commercial connection. PM Surya Ghar is only for residential connections, so a shop or office with its own commercial meter gets no subsidy. It can claim 40% accelerated depreciation instead if the business has taxable income; our calculator shows this as about 12% of the system cost. Small commercial systems up to 10 kW also need no technical feasibility study and are usually billed without a time-of-day tariff.
