Solar for EV Charging at Home: How Many kW for an Electric Car or Scooter

An electric car charged at home is usually the biggest load a household adds in years, often bigger than a new AC. This guide works out how many units your EV uses each month, how much rooftop solar covers it, what the PM Surya Ghar subsidy does and does not pay for, and why the time of day you charge matters as much as the size of the system.

Quick answer: an electric car uses about 16 units per 100 km, so 40 km a day is roughly 190 units a month. In Delhi that needs about 1.2 kW of extra panels. A Delhi home using 300 units a month goes from 1.9 kW to 3 kW when it adds that car, and because the subsidy keeps paying up to 3 kW, the net cost rises by about ₹52,902 and the whole system still pays back in about 4.8 years. An electric scooter is a small load: 40 km a day is about 42 units a month, or 0.3 kW of solar.

How many units an EV uses

EV consumption is quoted in units (kWh) per 100 km, or km per unit. 2026 Indian running-cost guides put most electric cars at about 13–18 units per 100 km at the meter in mixed driving (roughly 5.5–7.5 km per unit), including charging losses. This guide uses 16 units per 100 km. Electric scooters, with 2–5 kWh batteries, use about 3–4 units per 100 km; this guide uses 3.5. Your car's own trip computer, or the units your meter records over a month of charging, will be more accurate than either.

The subsidy explains most of the pattern. PM Surya Ghar pays ₹30,000 a kW for the first 2 kW and ₹18,000 for the third, and nothing beyond 3 kW (₹78,000 maximum). A 300-unit home on 1.9 kW still has subsidy room, so adding a car that does 40 km a day takes it to exactly 3 kW and the extra 1.1 kW costs about ₹52,902 after subsidy. That extra outlay is recovered in about 5.3 years from the extra ₹9,912 a year it saves. A 500-unit home is already at 3.1 kW, so the same car adds 1.1 kW at full price, ₹73,903, and that part takes about 7.5 years to pay back. Both are still reasonable, but the case for sizing up is strongest in smaller homes.

If you are buying the car within a year or two, it is usually cheaper to install the larger system once than to add panels later, which can mean a second structure, an inverter that is too small and another round of DISCOM approvals. The 3 kW vs 5 kW guide and the 5 kW guide cover the bigger sizes.

When you charge matters

A home on-grid system without a battery cannot charge your car after sunset. What it can do depends on your net metering arrangement:

  • Charging in daylight (weekends, work from home, a car that sits at home during the day) uses solar units directly. Each one saves your full tariff, ₹7 in Delhi.
  • Charging at night draws from the grid. The units your panels exported during the day are credited against your bill, but where exports are settled at a buyback rate rather than offset unit for unit, each exported unit is worth only ₹2–3.50.

The calculator assumes you use 65% of your solar units as they are made and export the rest. For the Delhi home plus car on 3 kW, that gives a first-year benefit of ₹27,195. If daytime charging raised that share to 85%, the same arithmetic gives about ₹30,500 a year and a payback of about 4.3 years instead of 4.8. The calculator's tip after each estimate makes the same point: shifting a load such as EV charging into daylight is often worth more than adding panels.

Two practical limits. A rooftop system rarely delivers its full rated kW, and output follows the sun, so a 3.3 kW charger running at noon will often draw part of its power from the grid; on an on-grid system that happens automatically. And a battery that stores solar for night charging adds a lot of cost; see hybrid solar for what a battery adds to payback.

Chargers, sanctioned load and tariffs

  • Charger type. Many cars come with a portable charger of about 3.3 kW that plugs into a dedicated socket. A 7.2 kW wall-mounted charger charges about twice as fast; 2026 guides put it at roughly ₹40,000–75,000 plus installation. For 30–60 km a day, a 3.3 kW charger refills the day's use in about 1.5–3 hours.
  • Sanctioned load. A charger adds several kW to your peak demand, and national net-metering rules allow rooftop solar only up to your sanctioned load. If you are adding both a charger and a larger system, check your sanctioned load first; a load enhancement from your DISCOM may be needed. The 10 kW guide covers single- and three-phase limits.
  • Tariff. Under the Ministry of Power's EV charging guidelines, you can charge at home on your existing connection, and the domestic tariff applies. Some states also have a separate EV tariff or time-of-day rates, which can change when charging is cheapest; check your DISCOM's current tariff order.
  • Flats and societies. If you park in a society basement, the charger usually runs off a common or separate meter. A housing society or RWA gets ₹18,000 per kW under PM Surya Ghar for solar that powers common facilities, which includes EV charging; see solar for housing societies.

In the calculator's appliance mode, the list already includes a home EV charger (3.3 kW for 4 hours a day, about 13 units a day, which is closer to 80 km a day). Set its hours to match your driving, about 2 hours for 40 km a day, or simply add the car's monthly units from the table above to your bill units in bill mode.

Frequently asked questions

How much solar do I need to charge an electric car at home?
About 1.2 kW of extra panels for a car driven 40 km a day in a state with 5.5 peak sun hours such as Delhi. At about 16 units per 100 km that is roughly 190 units a month, and the calculator sizes 1 kW for every 165 units a month there. Driving 20 km a day needs about 0.6 kW and 60 km a day about 1.8 kW. In less sunny states such as Kerala or West Bengal, add about a fifth.
How many units does an electric car use per month?
Most electric cars in India use about 13–18 units (kWh) per 100 km at the meter, including charging losses. At 16 units per 100 km, 30 km a day uses about 144 units a month, 40 km a day about 192 units and 60 km a day about 288 units. An electric scooter uses roughly 3–4 units per 100 km, so 40 km a day is only about 42 units a month.
Does the PM Surya Ghar subsidy increase if I add panels for an EV?
Only up to 3 kW. The household subsidy is ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW and ₹18,000 for the third kW, capped at ₹78,000. If your home alone already needs 3 kW or more, every kW you add for the car is paid in full, so the extra panels take longer to pay back. In Delhi they pay back in about 5.3 years for a 300-unit home and about 7.5 years for a 500-unit home.
Can I charge my EV from rooftop solar at night?
Not directly from an on-grid system without a battery; at night the car charges from the grid. Under net metering the units your panels exported during the day are credited against those night-time units, so the bill still falls. Charging during daylight is worth more, because each solar unit you use yourself saves your full tariff instead of earning the lower buyback rate.
